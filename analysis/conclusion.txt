==========================================================================================
  SOCIAL NETWORK ANALYSIS — EMAIL-EU-CORE TEMPORAL DATASET
  Descriptive Conclusion: Cross-Department Communication Patterns
==========================================================================================

1. DATASET & SOURCE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Source: the 'email-Eu-core-temporal' dataset from the Stanford Network Analysis Project
(SNAP), introduced in A. Paranjape, A. R. Benson and J. Leskovec, 'Motifs in Temporal
Networks', WSDM 2017. It records anonymised emails between members of a large European
research institution. The four department files (Dept1–Dept4) each contain only emails
sent between members of the same department; each line is one email: sender, recipient,
and seconds since that file's first email.

Each department is treated as an independent directed weighted graph: nodes are employees,
a directed edge u → v means u emailed v, and edge weight is the number of emails along
that channel. Node IDs are local to each department and cannot be compared across them.

Across all four departments the data covers 702 employees and 168,175 emails.

Time alignment: every department file starts its clock at its own first email, so the four
clocks are offset from each other. Cross-correlating each department's hourly email counts
against the full dataset shows the departments agree with each other to within 6 hours.
That is negligible at the day and month scales used below, so comparing departments month
by month is valid.

2. METHODS AND WHY THEY WERE CHOSEN
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  • Weighted directed graphs: email has a sender and a recipient, and repeated emails mean
  a stronger tie, so direction and weight are both kept.
  • Density and distinct contacts: measure cohesion: how much of the possible
  communication actually happens, and how many different colleagues each person deals
  with.
  • Weakly / strongly connected components: measure fragmentation (groups that never email
  each other at all) and directed reachability (whether information can flow round and
  back).
  • Reciprocity: measures two-way exchange directly: the share of ties u → v that are
  answered by v → u. Component size cannot show this, since a strong component only needs
  a directed cycle, not mutual replies.
  • Clustering coefficient: measures whether a person's contacts also email each other,
  distinguishing tight closed teams from broad hub-and-spoke networks.
  • Degree, betweenness and PageRank centrality: identify the most active people, the
  brokers who sit on paths between others (unweighted, because weights are counts, not
  distances), and the most influential people by weighted email flow.
  • Louvain community detection: finds sub-teams by maximising modularity. Run on the
  undirected weighted projection, since modularity is defined for undirected graphs, with
  a fixed seed for reproducibility.
  • Monthly slices with rolling Z-scores: find months that are unusually high or low
  relative to their neighbours (centred 5-month window), so slow trends do not mask short
  swings.
  • Daily counts: check the monthly view: 30-day bins with an arbitrary start can split or
  dilute week-long events.
  • Random baselines and permutation tests: check that results are not what chance alone
  would produce: clustering is compared with degree-preserving random rewirings of each
  network, reciprocity with the density (its value in a random graph), and the shared
  high/low months with randomly shifted versions of each department's monthly series.

3. NETWORK SIZE & EMAIL VOLUME
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Dept1: 309 employees, 3031 directed edges, 61,046 emails (198 per person, 20.1 per
  channel).
    Dept2: 162 employees, 1772 directed edges, 46,772 emails (289 per person, 26.4 per
  channel).
    Dept3: 89 employees, 1506 directed edges, 12,216 emails (137 per person, 8.1 per
  channel).
    Dept4: 142 employees, 1375 directed edges, 48,141 emails (339 per person, 35.0 per
  channel).

Dept1 is both the largest department (309 people) and the busiest (61,046 emails), so
total volume broadly follows headcount. Intensity is what differs: Dept4 averages 35
emails per channel and 339 per person, while Dept3 averages only 8 per channel. Dept4
concentrates its email in a few heavily used channels; Dept3 spreads lighter traffic
across many contacts.

4. DENSITY & CONNECTIVITY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Density is the fraction of all possible directed edges that actually exist (0–1).

  Dept3: density = 0.1923
  Dept4: density = 0.0687
  Dept2: density = 0.0679
  Dept1: density = 0.0318

Dept3 has the highest density (0.1923): about one in 5 possible email channels is used.
Dept1 is the sparsest (0.0318). Part of this is size, since possible edges grow with n²
while each person's contacts do not, so density is best read together with connectivity
below.

Weak connectivity (ignoring edge direction):
  Dept1: 9 components; largest covers 32.7% of employees
  Dept2: 6 components; largest covers 28.4% of employees
  Dept3: fully connected (single component)
  Dept4: 6 components; largest covers 26.8% of employees

Dept3 is the only department where everyone is connected to everyone else, directly or
indirectly. Dept1, Dept2, Dept4 each split into 6–9 separate groups that never email each
other within the department; the largest group covers only 27–33% of people.

5. DIRECTED REACHABILITY & RECIPROCITY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A strongly connected component (SCC) is a set of people who can all reach each other
following the direction of email. Reciprocity is the share of ties that are answered.

  Dept1: 81 SCCs (71 of them single people), largest = 27.8% of employees; reciprocity = 0.72; 55 people only receive
  Dept2: 45 SCCs (39 of them single people), largest = 19.8% of employees; reciprocity = 0.82; 30 people only receive
  Dept3: 11 SCCs (10 of them single people), largest = 88.8% of employees; reciprocity = 0.71; 10 people only receive
  Dept4: 24 SCCs (18 of them single people), largest = 23.9% of employees; reciprocity = 0.79; 16 people only receive

Reciprocity is high in every department (0.71–0.82): most email relationships are two-way
everywhere. In a random network with the same density, only 0.03–0.19 of ties would be
answered, so reciprocity is 4–23× what chance would give. The many SCCs in Dept1, Dept2,
Dept4 therefore do not mean email is mostly one-way. They come from the fragmentation
above plus peripheral members: in Dept1, 71 of its 81 SCCs are single people, and 55
people receive email from colleagues but never send any within the department (consistent
with distribution lists, or with people whose work happens outside it). Dept3's large SCC
(89%) follows from it being one connected group.

6. COMMUNICATION BREADTH & CLUSTERING
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Dept1: 12.5 distinct contacts per person; avg clustering = 0.57 (random baseline 0.10, 5.7× random)
  Dept2: 12.9 distinct contacts per person; avg clustering = 0.73 (random baseline 0.14, 5.3× random)
  Dept3: 21.9 distinct contacts per person; avg clustering = 0.52 (random baseline 0.45, 1.2× random)
  Dept4: 11.7 distinct contacts per person; avg clustering = 0.72 (random baseline 0.15, 4.8× random)

Dept3 people each deal with about 22 different colleagues, against roughly 12 elsewhere.
Yet Dept3 has the lowest clustering (0.52): its contacts span the whole department, so
many of a person's contacts do not email each other. Dept2 and Dept4 have the highest
clustering (0.72–0.73): their groups are tight teams where nearly everyone emails
everyone, but the teams are cut off from each other.

The random baselines confirm this. In Dept1, Dept2, Dept4 clustering is 4.8–5.7× what
randomly rewired networks with the same contact counts produce, so their tight teams are
real structure. In Dept3 it is only 1.16× random: its clustering is almost entirely
explained by its high density, with little extra closed-team structure.

7. COMMUNITY STRUCTURE — LOUVAIN MODULARITY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Modularity Q measures how strongly a network divides into communities; values above about
0.3 are commonly taken to indicate meaningful community structure (Newman & Girvan, 2004).

  Dept1: 11 communities, Q = 0.7932, largest = 32.7% of employees, 10% of people have ties to another community
  Dept2: 7 communities, Q = 0.6935, largest = 19.8% of employees, 22% of people have ties to another community
  Dept3: 6 communities, Q = 0.4295, largest = 29.2% of employees, 94% of people have ties to another community
  Dept4: 6 communities, Q = 0.7282, largest = 26.8% of employees, 0% of people have ties to another community

Interpretive caveat: Dept1, Dept2, Dept4 are split into disconnected components, and
Louvain assigns each component to its own community at no cost, so their high Q
(0.69–0.79) mostly reflects that disconnection. Their communities-per-component ratios
(1.00–1.22) confirm that almost every community is simply one component; in Dept4 the
match is exact, with 0% of people tied to another community.

Dept3 is the analytically meaningful case: one connected network in which Louvain still
finds 6 communities with Q = 0.43, above the 0.3 rule of thumb. 94% of its people have
ties into another community, so these are distinct sub-teams that stay in contact with
each other.

8. KEY PEOPLE — CENTRALITY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PageRank (weighted) ranks influence through email flow; betweenness ranks brokers on the
shortest paths between others; degree counts distinct ties. Node IDs are local, so only
patterns, not people, are compared across departments.

  Dept1: top PageRank = node 279, top betweenness = node 279 (0.016); top 5 hold 25% of betweenness, 18% of email volume; 31% are never on a shortest path
  Dept2: top PageRank = node 24, top betweenness = node 63 (0.004); top 5 hold 25% of betweenness, 27% of email volume; 31% are never on a shortest path
  Dept3: top PageRank = node 60, top betweenness = node 60 (0.066); top 5 hold 33% of betweenness, 22% of email volume; 14% are never on a shortest path
  Dept4: top PageRank = node 72, top betweenness = node 111 (0.006); top 5 hold 24% of betweenness, 25% of email volume; 25% are never on a shortest path

Dept1 has one dominant hub: node 279 is first on degree, betweenness and PageRank, with a
PageRank 2.1× the next person's. All five top brokers sit in the department's largest
community (101 people), so brokerage happens inside that group, not between groups. This
is a single point of failure for Dept1's main group.

Dept2 is led by a small core: nodes 24, 63, 91 are in the top five on all three measures,
and node 24 handles 8,594 emails sent and received, the most in the department.

Dept3 has real brokers: its top betweenness (0.066) is 4× the highest anywhere else, and
nodes 25, 60 rank top five on all three measures. These are the people connecting its sub-
teams. Only 14% of Dept3 is never on a shortest path, versus 25–31% elsewhere, so most
people take some part in passing information on.

Dept4 has no single leader: its top five PageRank scores are nearly equal (the first is
only 1.2× the fifth), matching a set of self-contained teams of similar weight.

Caveat: betweenness is normalised over all pairs of people, and no shortest paths cross
between disconnected groups, so Dept1, Dept2, Dept4 score lower partly by construction.
The comparison of shares and patterns above is more reliable than the raw values.

9. TEMPORAL ANALYSIS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The data is continuous from day 0 to day 527, then has no email at all until day 797,
after which only 7 more days are recorded. Events were split into 30-day slices (M01, M02,
…). Only complete months are analysed (M01–M17): the slice the data stops in (M18) and the
7-day fragment at the end are partial, and their low counts reflect missing days, not
quiet periods.

  Monthly email volume (complete months):
  Dept1: mean=3414, std=1065, min=1605, max=5061 emails/month
  Dept2: mean=2602, std=893, min=1024, max=4019 emails/month
  Dept3: mean=682, std=244, min=155, max=1079 emails/month
  Dept4: mean=2707, std=886, min=562, max=4091 emails/month

  High / low months (Z-score beyond ±1.0 against a centred 5-month rolling mean):
  Dept1: high → ['M08', 'M13']   low → ['M03', 'M10', 'M14']
  Dept2: high → ['M08', 'M13']   low → ['M03', 'M10', 'M14']
  Dept3: high → ['M08']   low → ['M03', 'M10', 'M14']
  Dept4: high → ['M08', 'M13']   low → ['M03', 'M10', 'M14']

All four departments rise and fall together: M08 is high in every department and M03, M10,
M14 are low in every department. M13 is high in three departments; in Dept3 it scores Z =
0.997, just under the cut-off. Since the departments are separate networks, this shared
rhythm points to institution-wide causes such as calendars, deadlines and holidays.

This synchrony is statistically significant. The ±1 cut-off flags 28% of department-
months, so any single department's flags could be chance. But 4 months are flagged the
same way in all four departments, against 0.02 expected when each department's series is
shifted at random (p = 0.0006, 10,000 permutations).

In M14 every department drops sharply: Dept1 falls to 1,683 emails (49% of its monthly
average), Dept2 falls to 1,024 emails (39% of its monthly average), Dept3 falls to 155
emails (23% of its monthly average), Dept4 falls to 562 emails (21% of its monthly
average).

Monthly Z-scores stay between -1.72 and +1.34: month to month, volume moves within a
moderate band. The large swings are week-scale, as the daily view shows.

  Daily rhythm:
  Weekend days carry only 8% of a weekday's email, confirming this is work communication.
  The weekly view reveals what monthly bins blur:
    • M08's peak is one intense week: the 7 days around day 238 carry 2.3× a median week's
    email.
    • Low periods (below half the median week): days 61–77 (M03, 33% of normal); days
    186–195 (M07, 36% of normal); days 278–294 (M10, 49% of normal); days 393–422
    (M14/M15, 31% of normal); days 431–440 (M15, 35% of normal).
    • The longest break lasts 30 days and straddles M14 and M15, so monthly bins split it.
    The low periods in M07, M15 do not show up in the monthly analysis at all.
    • Breaks starting on days 61 and 431 are 370 days apart, which suggests a recurring
    annual holiday. The actual calendar dates cannot be recovered, because timestamps are
    relative.

  Structural metrics over time (complete months):
  Dept1: avg active people/month=216 (of 309), avg degree/month=7.52, density M01=0.0170 → M17=0.0201
  Dept2: avg active people/month=114 (of 162), avg degree/month=10.29, density M01=0.0433 → M17=0.0505
  Dept3: avg active people/month=68 (of 89), avg degree/month=8.13, density M01=0.0518 → M17=0.0705
  Dept4: avg active people/month=100 (of 142), avg degree/month=8.87, density M01=0.0437 → M17=0.0482

In any given month only part of each department is active, so the static networks above
combine people whose activity is spread across the whole period.

11. KEY FINDINGS & INTERPRETATION
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  1. Dept3 is the most cohesive department, organised around brokers rather than cliques.
     It is the only fully connected department, with the highest density (0.19) and the
     most contacts per person (22). Its low clustering and strong brokers show a broad
     network held together by a few well-connected people, with 6 sub-teams that stay in
     contact.

  2. Dept1, Dept2 and Dept4 are collections of separate tight teams.
     Each splits into 6–9 groups that never email each other within the department. Inside
     those groups, clustering is 4.8–5.7× what random networks give: genuinely close-knit
     teams with little contact between them.

  3. Two-way communication is the norm everywhere.
     Reciprocity is 0.71–0.82 in all four departments, 4–23× chance. Dept1's many strong
     components come from fragmentation and receive-only members, not from one-way email.

  4. Departments differ in communication intensity.
     Dept4 sends 35 emails per channel against Dept3's 8: a few heavy channels versus many
     light ones.

  5. High modularity in Dept1, Dept2 and Dept4 is largely an artefact.
     Their Q values (0.69–0.79) mostly count already-disconnected components as
     communities. Dept3's lower Q is the only one reflecting community structure inside
     one connected network.

  6. Influence is concentrated differently in each department.
     Dept1 depends on one hub (node 279); Dept2 on a core of 3 people; Dept3 on a few
     brokers between sub-teams; Dept4 has no single leader.

  7. All departments follow the same institutional calendar.
     M08 is high and M03, M10, M14 are low in every department. The department clocks
     agree to within 6 hours, and a permutation test puts the chance of this much overlap
     at p = 0.0006.

  8. The real swings are week-long.
     The M08 peak is a single week at 2.3× normal, and the dips are breaks of 10–30 days
     where email drops to 31–49% of normal, including one that recurs about a year later.
     Weekends carry only 8% of a weekday's email.

12. LIMITATIONS & FUTURE WORK
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  • Only email within each department is included, so links between departments are
  invisible. Groups that look isolated may be connected through other departments.
  • Email is a proxy for collaboration. Volume may include distribution-list or automated
  messages, and without message content these cannot be separated out.
  • The data is anonymised, with no roles or org chart, so communities and hubs cannot be
  checked against real teams or positions.
  • Timestamps are relative, so calendar dates (and the cause of each peak or break) are
  unknown. The 30-day bins are arbitrary, and the data has a long gap after day 527.
  • Betweenness is not directly comparable between connected and fragmented networks.
  Louvain is randomised; across 20 seeds Q varies by at most 0.017 and the number of
  communities by at most 1, so the conclusions do not depend on the seed.

  Future work:
  • Analyse the full dataset to see how departments connect to each other.
  • Study temporal motifs (recurring patterns of who emails whom in sequence), the purpose
  the dataset was published for.
  • Track communities month by month to see whether teams merge, split or persist, and
  test how well past contact predicts future links.

13. CONCLUSION
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This analysis combines static network structure, centrality, community detection and
temporal dynamics to compare email communication in four departments of a European
research institution over 528 days of continuous data.

Structurally, the departments follow two different models. Dept3 is one connected, broad
network held together by a few brokers, with sub-teams that stay in contact. Dept1, Dept2
and Dept4 are each made up of several close-knit teams that never email each other within
the department; their high modularity reflects that separation rather than well-formed
sub-groups. Communication is reciprocal in all four, so the difference lies in how the
teams connect, not in whether people reply.

Temporally, all four departments share one rhythm: the same high and low months, a single
surge week, multi-week breaks (one recurring about a year later) and quiet weekends.
Communication volume is driven by institution-wide schedules more than by anything
department-specific.

Implications for the organisation: Dept1's isolated teams are the clearest candidates for
deliberate bridging, and its reliance on node 279 is a continuity risk. Dept3 shows what a
well-connected department looks like, but it too depends on a handful of brokers. The
shared break periods are natural windows for planned changes, and the surge week shows
when the whole institution is under the most communication load.
